# Self-Healing Mortar with Superabsorbent Polymers

### A Fracture Mechanics Study of Dry and Calcium-Loaded SAP Systems

Experimental investigation of self-healing mortar incorporating superabsorbent polymers (SAP), evaluated using semi-circular bending (SCB) fracture testing.

This study compares conventional mortar with dry SAP and calcium-loaded (limewater-loaded) SAP systems to investigate how SAP influences crack resistance, fracture behavior, and post-damage performance.

> Presented at the Symposium on Sustainable Construction Materials at the University of Nebraska–Lincoln, where the project received First Prize.

---

## Overview

Cracking is one of the primary mechanisms that reduces the durability and service life of cementitious materials.

Even relatively small cracks can allow water, chlorides, and other aggressive agents to penetrate the material, potentially accelerating deterioration and increasing future repair requirements.

Superabsorbent polymers are cross-linked hydrophilic materials capable of absorbing and later releasing water within a cementitious matrix. This behavior can support internal curing, continued hydration, and crack-sealing mechanisms.

This project investigated three mortar systems:

- **Control Mix** — conventional mortar without SAP
- **Dry SAP Mix** — mortar containing dry superabsorbent polymer
- **Calcium-Loaded SAP Mix** — SAP pre-soaked in limewater before incorporation

The primary objective was not simply to determine which mixture produced the highest strength, but to evaluate how each material behaved after controlled damage and a subsequent healing period.

---

## Research Questions

The study focused on several related questions:

1. How does dry SAP influence early-age fracture behavior?
2. How does calcium-loaded SAP compare with conventional dry SAP?
3. How much load-carrying capacity remains after controlled cracking and healing?
4. How much fracture-energy capacity is retained following damage?
5. Do strength recovery and toughness recovery follow the same trend?

---

## Experimental Methodology

The experimental program used **semi-circular bending (SCB) testing** to evaluate fracture response.

### Mortar Systems

| Mix | Description |
|---|---|
| Control | Conventional mortar without SAP |
| Dry SAP | Mortar containing dry superabsorbent polymer |
| Calcium-Loaded SAP | SAP pre-soaked in limewater prior to mixing |

### Mix Proportions

The experimental mortar used approximately:

- Cement : Sand = **1 : 2.75**
- Water-to-cement ratio ≈ **0.48**
- SAP dosage = **0.15% of cement mass**

---

## Experimental Sequence

```text
Mixing
   ↓
Casting
   ↓
Curing
   ↓
Cutting Semi-Circular Specimens
   ↓
Day 7 Baseline SCB Testing
   ↓
Preloading to 60% of Peak Load
   ↓
Healing from Day 7 to Day 14
   ↓
Day 14 SCB Testing
   ↓
Fracture and Retention Analysis
```

Selected specimens were preloaded to **60% of peak load at Day 7** to introduce controlled microcracking without causing complete failure.

The damaged specimens were then exposed to moisture and allowed to heal from **Day 7 to Day 14** before final SCB testing.

The final analysis compared:

- Day 7 baseline behavior
- Day 14 specimens without intentional preloading
- Day 14 specimens that were preloaded and allowed to heal

This experimental framework allowed normal curing effects, controlled damage, and post-damage healing response to be evaluated separately.

---

## Parameters Evaluated

The SCB load–deformation response was used to investigate:

- Peak load
- Deformation at failure
- Load–deformation behavior
- Energy absorption
- Estimated fracture energy
- Retained peak load
- Retained fracture energy
- Relative brittleness and toughness

Fracture energy was estimated from the area under the load–deformation curve and normalized using the ligament area of the SCB specimen.

The analysis considered both strength-related and toughness-related behavior because peak load alone does not fully describe post-cracking material performance.

## Estimated Fracture Energy

![Fracture Energy Comparison](./fracture_energy_grouped_by_mix_condition.png)

| Mix | 7 Day | 14 Day — Not Preloaded | 14 Day — Preloaded |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Control | 0.106 kJ/m² | 0.177 kJ/m² | 0.100 kJ/m² |
| Dry SAP | 0.105 kJ/m² | 0.139 kJ/m² | 0.132 kJ/m² |
| Calcium-Loaded SAP | 0.127 kJ/m² | 0.124 kJ/m² | 0.084 kJ/m² |

The Control Mix produced the highest fracture-energy value in the undamaged 14-day condition.

However, following controlled damage, the Dry SAP mixture showed substantially better preservation of fracture-energy capacity.

---

## Post-Damage Retention

| Mix | Retained Peak Load | Retained Fracture Energy |
|---|---:|---:|
| Control | 92.5% | 56.5% |
| Dry SAP | 92.9% | 95.0% |
| Calcium-Loaded SAP | 93.3% | 67.7% |

### Key Finding

The results demonstrate an important distinction between **strength recovery** and **toughness retention**.

The Calcium-Loaded SAP mixture produced the highest retained peak load, while the Dry SAP mixture demonstrated substantially greater retained fracture energy.

This indicates that a material can preserve a high percentage of its peak load capacity while still exhibiting a comparatively more brittle fracture response.

For this reason, evaluating self-healing cementitious materials using strength alone may not fully describe their post-damage behavior.

---

# Engineering Interpretation

## Dry SAP

Dry SAP demonstrated the strongest preservation of fracture-energy capacity following controlled damage.

Potential contributing mechanisms include:

- Internal moisture storage and release
- Continued cement hydration
- Formation of additional hydration products near crack regions
- Reduced crack connectivity
- Improved resistance to crack reopening and propagation

The Dry SAP system retained approximately **95% of its fracture-energy response** relative to the corresponding undamaged 14-day condition.

This suggests improved preservation of post-cracking toughness.

---

## Calcium-Loaded SAP

The calcium-loaded system showed the highest retained peak load but lower fracture-energy retention than Dry SAP.

The limewater treatment was intended to provide both moisture and calcium ions to damaged regions.

Released calcium ions may contribute to calcium-based healing mechanisms, including the possible formation of calcium carbonate within crack regions.

However, the present experiment did not directly characterize the chemical composition of the healing products.

The mechanical results therefore suggest a potentially stronger but more localized and comparatively brittle recovery response.

---

# Strength vs. Toughness

One of the most important findings from the study was that peak-load recovery and fracture-energy recovery did not necessarily follow the same trend.

A specimen can exhibit:

- High retained peak load
- Strong resistance to initial loading
- Lower fracture-energy retention
- More brittle post-peak behavior

Conversely, another material may exhibit slightly lower peak-load recovery while preserving substantially more energy absorption during crack propagation.

This distinction is important when evaluating self-healing and damage-resistant cementitious systems.

Material performance after cracking should therefore be evaluated using multiple parameters rather than relying on a single strength measurement.

---

# Why This Matters

Cracking can reduce concrete durability by creating pathways for moisture and aggressive ions to enter the material.

Self-healing cementitious systems have the potential to reduce the effects of cracking throughout the service life of infrastructure.

Potential benefits include:

- Improved crack control
- Reduced moisture ingress
- Reduced chloride ingress
- Improved durability
- Longer service life
- Fewer repair interventions
- Reduced material consumption
- Reduced construction waste
- Lower lifecycle maintenance requirements
- Reduced lifecycle environmental impact

The project also demonstrates the importance of **fracture-based evaluation** when investigating materials intended to continue performing after cracking.

# Limitations

This study should be interpreted as an early-stage experimental investigation.

Important limitations include:

- Limited sample size
- Relatively short healing period
- Variability inherent in cementitious materials
- Sensitivity of SCB results to specimen geometry and alignment
- Some subjectivity associated with trimming post-failure load–deformation data
- Fracture energy was used primarily as a comparative parameter
- Healing products were not chemically or microscopically characterized

The results therefore indicate comparative experimental trends rather than definitive long-term material performance.

---

# Future Work

Future research could expand the study through:

- Larger specimen populations
- 28-day and longer healing periods
- Additional SAP dosages
- Alternative calcium-loading conditions
- Wet–dry healing cycles
- Crack-width measurement
- Microscopy of healed crack regions
- Chemical characterization of healing products
- Sorptivity testing
- Durability testing
- Chloride-ingress evaluation
- Statistical analysis of specimen variability

These extensions could help determine whether the observed short-term mechanical trends translate into meaningful long-term durability improvements.
